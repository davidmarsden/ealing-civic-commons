# Charter of the Commons

**Status:** Founding network charter  
**Home:** https://civiccommons.co.uk/charter/

## Preamble

Local knowledge is public infrastructure.

Every place accumulates a public memory: reporting, records, minutes, notices, campaigns, decisions, testimony, photographs, research, corrections and the connections between them. Too often that memory is scattered across websites, feeds, inboxes and institutions, then lost when a publication closes, a meeting passes, a link breaks or a platform changes.

Civic Commons exists to keep useful local public knowledge visible, connected and held in common.

The name deliberately recalls an older tradition. The commons were never just land. The Charter of the Forest of 1217 protected customary rights to shared resources against enclosure by power. A Civic Commons applies the same broad principle to local public knowledge: what people need in order to understand, remember and participate in the civic life of a place should not disappear behind unnecessary barriers or be enclosed by a single publisher, institution or platform.

This charter is not a licence to copy other people’s work. It is a commitment to keep provenance visible, point back to original sources, preserve public memory and build civic infrastructure that remains accountable to the place it serves.

## 1. The record belongs in public view

A Civic Commons should make useful local public information easier to find, understand and revisit.

It should preserve the civic trail after the news cycle, meeting, campaign or controversy has moved on. Stable links, archives, source records and durable relationships matter because public memory should not depend on what happens to be prominent today.

## 2. Original sources remain canonical

A Civic Commons connects the record; it does not replace the people and organisations that created it.

Original journalism, official records, community publications, research, notices, videos and other source material should remain clearly attributed and linked. The Commons may preserve metadata, context and civic relationships, but it should not silently present somebody else’s work as its own.

Provenance is part of the public record, not technical clutter to be hidden.

## 3. Connect, do not flatten

Civic life is made of relationships: between people, organisations, places, decisions, documents, stories, issues and time.

A Civic Commons should help readers move through those relationships without erasing their differences. Journalism is not the same thing as an official notice; an allegation is not the same thing as a finding; a historical organisation is not automatically a current one.

Context should connect records without flattening them into false equivalence.

## 4. Chronology before popularity

The Commons is civic memory, not an attention market.

Public material should not be ranked primarily by outrage, clicks, advertising value or engagement. Chronology, relevance to place, provenance and reviewed civic relationships should do the organising work wherever possible.

A Civic Commons may help people discover what matters, but it should not manufacture importance through opaque behavioural ranking.

## 5. Participation is reviewed, not popularity-ranked

Residents and communities should be able to add corrections, evidence, context, related sources and local knowledge.

Participation does not mean that every submission immediately becomes reviewed civic knowledge. Public additions should pass through a visible, accountable review boundary. Suggestions can propose knowledge; they must not silently rewrite the record.

Moderation should protect accuracy, privacy and fairness without turning civic participation into a popularity contest.

## 6. Private people are not raw material

A Civic Commons should minimise the private data it collects and separate private submission information from public civic records.

No behavioural advertising. No sale of personal data. No requirement to join a closed social platform merely to read or participate. Identity requirements should be introduced only where they solve a real civic or moderation problem.

The public interest does not remove the obligation to handle personal information carefully.

## 7. Open doors, not walled gardens

The network should be useful through ordinary public web links and open formats wherever practical.

RSS, portable feeds, durable URLs, accessible documents and interoperable standards are preferred to platform lock-in. External social networks and protocols may connect to a Civic Commons, but they should not own its public memory or become prerequisites for access.

The Commons should remain useful when any one external service disappears.

## 8. Built locally

Each Civic Commons belongs to its place.

Local organisers choose the sources, boundaries, names, institutions, issues and presentation that make sense locally. A Civic Commons should not impose a single editorial voice, visual identity or civic geography from elsewhere.

Local knowledge requires local judgement.

## 9. A network, not a franchise

Civic Commons is a shared model, not a chain.

No locality needs permission from a central organisation to build civic infrastructure around these principles. The purpose of the network is to connect, document and learn from local Commons, not to own them.

Shared network identity can help people recognise the model, while local identity should remain rooted in the place itself.

## 10. Power should be legible

A Civic Commons should make it easier to see how decisions, institutions, ownership, responsibility and public consequences connect.

That does not mean assuming bad faith. It means refusing to let administrative fragmentation make accountability impossible to follow.

The system should help answer not only “what happened?” but also “what happened to this place?”, “who decided?”, “what record exists?” and “what connects this to what came before?”

## 11. Memory should outlast projects

Local civic knowledge should not vanish because a campaign ends, an organisation closes, a volunteer leaves or a software service changes its terms.

A Civic Commons should favour exportable data, documented structures, resilient archives and clear stewardship so that its useful public record can survive the project that assembled it.

## 12. The Commons can be forked, improved and challenged

No implementation of these principles is final.

Local Commons should document how they work, expose their public rules and be open to correction. Other communities should be able to learn from, adapt and improve the model without inheriting every local choice made by the first implementation.

The first working Civic Commons is in Ealing. It is an example, not a template that every place must copy exactly.

## The compact promise

A Civic Commons should:

- **Remember civically** — keep useful local evidence findable.
- **Connect the record** — show relationships without erasing provenance or difference.
- **Keep sources visible** — send people back to the reporting, records and communities behind the picture.
- **Participate openly** — invite contribution through accountable review rather than engagement ranking.
- **Stay locally rooted** — let each place define its own civic geography and identity.
- **Remain open** — use durable public links and portable formats, and resist enclosure by any one platform or institution.

**Publish anywhere. Connect locally. Participate openly. Remember civically.**
